Training an NH AI opponent for browser PvP practice

NH Trainer is a browser practice client for fighting a trained sparring partner. This blog documents the practical work behind NH AI training, OSRS-style PvP practice, policy checkpoints, and the process of making the opponent more useful over time.

Why the bot uses a learned policy

NH fights are too reactive for a fixed rotation to stay interesting. The opponent needs to adapt to timing, pressure, and the player's rhythm rather than simply looping through a script.

The training process uses many paired fights and records a rolling window of recent combat state. That short memory lets rewards connect an action to future outcomes: a good freeze, a defensive gear swap, a delayed special attack, or a supply decision may only prove useful several ticks later.

What changed with the neural hard mode

Hard mode is now controlled by neural-network models for both setups. The selected setup decides which model loads: NH stake uses the larger continuation model, and DMM uses the newer loop-four DMM checkpoint. The bot still reads practical NH state instead of private hidden information: prayers, health, supplies, distance, freeze state, weapon timing, special attack state, recent pressure, and visible gear context.

  • NH stake Hard: 11,604,230 parameters, three 256-unit hidden layers, 90 inputs, 139 encoded features, 44,550 action outputs.
  • DMM Hard: 5,785,234 parameters, three 256-unit hidden layers, 92 inputs, 141 encoded features, 21,906 action outputs.
  • DMM can use style loadouts, equip weapons without immediately attacking, and explore selected helm unequips.
  • NH stake has the same idea for dragon boots: the model can learn whether taking them off is ever worth it from fight outcomes, not from a hand-written reward.

How I trained this version

The current direction is self-play. I train in loops, usually in groups of four, and feed the next data run with the best available version of the model fighting itself. That is important because I do not want the bot to only copy one fixed opponent or one old opponent. I still keep cohort fights around as pressure tests so the model does not forget common patterns while it learns from itself.

The cohorts are scripted styles used for evaluation and coverage: melee camping, repeated AGS spec pressure, mage-range alternation, mage-heavy and range-heavy pressure, staff or crossbow baiting, prayer-camp melee, panic eating, stand-under freeze, long-range crossbow, close fake switches, one-tick and two-tick fakes, and delayed reactions. They are not the final goal. They are checks that catch obvious regressions while the neural model learns a wider fight.

Tiny signals are the interesting part

One thing I kept thinking about was why the DMM model was not clearly learning to take off Torva helm for magic. I did a mechanics-only simulation with Augury on and the same opponent setup, then compared a large number of casts with the helm on versus off. The edge was real, but tiny: roughly a tenth of a damage per cast in expected value.

That is the kind of signal I am curious about now. I do not want to hard-code "take the helm off" or "take the boots off" as a reward. I want the model to find those edges because the outcomes are better over enough fights. The interesting question is how much training it needs before it notices something that small, especially when the wrong timing can make the same action bad.

Fixing what the bot was learning from

Some of the early problems were not model size problems. They were measurement problems. One old reward path tried to infer damage from HP changes. That breaks as soon as food is involved, because a player can eat on the same tick they take damage and end up with a similar HP value. The training signal then says the hit did not really happen, even though it did. That had to be fixed by using the actual damage events instead of only looking at the final HP number.

Another issue was timing. If the bot sees a weapon, movement, or prayer change too early, it becomes unfair and also learns the wrong fight. It can pray or switch on the same tick as the player's choice, which makes it feel impossible instead of good. I have been tightening the observation timing so the neural model learns from the information a player could actually react to.

What I am watching next

The next interesting thing for me is whether more DMM training makes the model time vengeance trinket usage better, especially around specs and predicted burst windows. I am also curious whether the neural model starts abusing worn-gear weaknesses more naturally: not because I told it a specific item trick, but because it learns when an equipment penalty or bonus actually changes the outcome of a fight.

Difficulty setup

Older builds exposed several checkpoint difficulties. The current public build is simpler: Hard is the supported opponent, and the NH stake or DMM setup determines which neural weights are loaded.
