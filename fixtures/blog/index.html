Training an NH AI opponent for browser PvP practice

NH Trainer is a browser practice client for fighting a trained sparring partner. This blog documents the practical work behind NH AI training, OSRS-style PvP practice, policy checkpoints, and the process of making the opponent more useful over time.

August 2026 - the Elo league, and why teacher165 earned the spot

The big thing that happened since the last post is that I finally built a proper ranking system. For a long time I was comparing checkpoints in small matched screens, which works but only ever tells you "better than the one it fought". I wanted a real ladder: everyone fights everyone, once, and the numbers come out of that.

So I ran one. Every compatible checkpoint I had plus the scripted opponents, all in one round-robin league: 313 entrants, every pair played one full fight, 48,828 fights in total, classic Elo with a K-factor of 32 and everyone starting at 1500. Nothing fancy - it's the boring kind of Elo - but it turns a pile of "probably fine" checkpoints into an actual ordering.

  • 1st - teacher139-no-opponent-prayer-all - 1890.6
  • 2nd - teacher111-prayer-vlsordinary-from110-alpha075 - 1833.3
  • 3rd - teacher165-ranged-multigear-prayer-from154 - 1797.2
  • 4th - teacher116b-safe-ward-from116a - 1792.9
  • 5th - lr050-ancestry20-r01 - 1790.4

The interesting part is that the league winner is not the one I shipped. First place is teacher139, and its name tells you exactly what's wrong with it: it's an ablation that literally cannot see the opponent's prayer. It still wins fights - it puts out 95.4 expected damage per fight and went 245-67 - but it only prays correctly 52% of the time. It wins on raw output, not on reading. That's not a useful practice opponent, that's a DPS check.

Second place (teacher111, 1833.3) is closer to being good: 79.2% prayer correctness and a 92.1 expected-damage edge per fight. But teacher165, one spot lower on rating, does the things I actually care about better: 84.9% prayer correctness - the best of the top five - with a 91.1 expected-damage edge and the best realized edge of the top three (+94.6). And its attack mix is properly balanced at 66% magic / 13% ranged / 21% melee instead of leaning on one style. Rating-wise the gap between 2nd and 3rd is tiny. On the things that make an opponent worth practicing against, 165 wins. So 165 is the model on the site now.

There's also a practical reason: teacher165 is from the newest training line, and it's the one with the browser contract actually verified against the Java checkpoint - I replayed real Java fight snapshots through both and they agree within 1e-5. A model that ranks 3rd in a simulator but provably behaves identically in the browser beats a model that ranks 1st and might not.

The road to teacher165

Since the last update I've been chasing one main thing: making the DMM bot read the fight properly instead of just outputting damage. The line from teacher148 to teacher165 went through a ranged multi-gear curriculum - teaching the bot to mix ranged pressure and believable staff/VLS gear fakes into its normal three-style openings - and a complete-history prayer head that reads a longer window of recent ticks instead of just the current one.

There were real hiccups along the way, and I'm not going to pretend they didn't happen.

The most humbling one: I trained a focused prayer head that looked amazing offline - 66% accuracy on untouched data, up from 19% - and then it completely failed to transfer to live fights, praying correctly 13.4% of the time. It had memorized the training distribution instead of learning timing. That whole branch got rejected. It taught me to stop trusting offline numbers that aren't backed by live fights.

Another one was quieter and nastier: a normalization repair I'd written earlier had silently zeroed five learned state inputs (attack, strength, ranged, magic ratios and magic deficit). The bot could still fight, but it had lost part of the information it needed to tell a boosted state from a drained one. That explained a confusing regression where a "broad improvement" run raised ranged usage while making the bot strictly worse at everything else.

And the elo league itself caught a degenerate case I'd have shipped otherwise: the #1-ranked checkpoint turns out to ignore opponent prayer entirely. Ratings are great, but they rank fighters, not practice partners. That's why the league became part of the selection process instead of the whole process.

The site also got simpler: I removed the old aim trainer section so the page is just the NH practice client with the NH stake and DMM setups. And the GPU fight engine that runs the league is now in the repo too, under fastsim/, so the whole evaluation path is visible next to the site.

Why the bot uses a learned policy

NH fights are too reactive for a fixed rotation to stay interesting. The opponent needs to adapt to timing, pressure, and the player's rhythm rather than simply looping through a script.

The training process uses many paired fights and records a rolling window of recent combat state. That short memory lets rewards connect an action to future outcomes: a good freeze, a defensive gear swap, a delayed special attack, or a supply decision may only prove useful several ticks later.

What changed with the neural hard mode

Hard mode is now controlled by neural-network models for both setups. The selected setup decides which model loads: NH stake and DMM each use their own current hard fixture. The bot still reads practical NH state instead of private hidden information: prayers, health, supplies, distance, freeze state, weapon timing, special attack state, recent pressure, and visible gear context.

  • NH stake Hard and DMM Hard are separate neural fixtures. Exact schema and parameter counts should be read from the current fixture files, not copied from old notes.
  • DMM hard uses direct item/slot actions rather than broad style loadouts: weapon equips, shield swaps, selected unequips/re-equips, and explicit Gmaul, Voidwaker, and Vesta's longsword spec actions.
  • Legacy compact DMM controllers, generic best-special fallbacks, and EV style overrides are no longer the intended hard-mode path.
  • NH stake has the same idea for dragon boots: the model can learn whether taking them off is ever worth it from fight outcomes, not from a hand-written reward.

How I trained this version

The current direction is self-play. I train in loops, usually in groups of four, and feed the next data run with the best available version of the model fighting itself. That is important because I do not want the bot to only copy one fixed opponent or one old opponent. I still keep cohort fights around as pressure tests so the model does not forget common patterns while it learns from itself.

Current DMM rollout data keeps one row per real bot decision. Same-tick direct gear choices are stored as labels on that row, so the model can learn multiple gear actions in one tick without pretending that extra game ticks happened.

The cohorts are scripted styles used for evaluation and coverage: melee camping, repeated AGS spec pressure, mage-range alternation, mage-heavy and range-heavy pressure, staff or crossbow baiting, prayer-camp melee, panic eating, stand-under freeze, long-range crossbow, close fake switches, one-tick and two-tick fakes, and delayed reactions. They are not the final goal. They are checks that catch obvious regressions while the neural model learns a wider fight.

Tiny signals are the interesting part

One thing I kept thinking about was why the DMM model was not clearly learning to take off Torva helm for magic. I did a mechanics-only simulation with Augury on and the same opponent setup, then compared a large number of casts with the helm on versus off. The edge was real, but tiny: roughly a tenth of a damage per cast in expected value.

That is the kind of signal I am curious about now. I do not want to hard-code "take the helm off" or "take the boots off" as a reward. I want the model to find those edges because the outcomes are better over enough fights. The interesting question is how much training it needs before it notices something that small, especially when the wrong timing can make the same action bad.

Fixing what the bot was learning from

Some of the early problems were not model size problems. They were measurement problems. One old reward path tried to infer damage from HP changes. That breaks as soon as food is involved, because a player can eat on the same tick they take damage and end up with a similar HP value. The training signal then says the hit did not really happen, even though it did. That had to be fixed by using the actual damage events instead of only looking at the final HP number.

Another issue was timing. If the bot sees a weapon, movement, or prayer change too early, it becomes unfair and also learns the wrong fight. It can pray or switch on the same tick as the player's choice, which makes it feel impossible instead of good. I have been tightening the observation timing so the neural model learns from the information a player could actually react to.

What I am watching next

The next interesting thing for me is whether more DMM training makes the model time vengeance trinket usage better, especially around specs and predicted burst windows. I am also curious whether the neural model starts abusing worn-gear weaknesses more naturally: not because I told it a specific item trick, but because it learns when an equipment penalty or bonus actually changes the outcome of a fight.

Difficulty setup

Older builds exposed several checkpoint difficulties. The current public build is simpler: Hard is the supported opponent, and the NH stake or DMM setup determines which neural weights are loaded.
